In Epicurus's letter to Pythocles, he proposes three different explanations for lightning strikes: compression of air by wind, a cloud suddenly breaking (he seems to imagine clouds as solid or at least sponge-like), or a buildup of fire (which he seems to imagine as a substance) suddenly bursting.

I suppose it's not any of these, it's... something to do with electricity?
Though I'm not sure why electricity does that.

But in any case, after giving his three explanations, Epicurus goes on to say (Makridis translation):

> It is indeed possible that the thunderbolt may be generated in any one of many more ways.

Maybe this is all you need if you're annoyed with people saying it's Zeus's wrath and they can tell you how to avoid it.
You just have to say, well, it could be a lot of other things too.

## Meeting unfounded explanations with more explanations

Maybe I used to think you shouldn't propose explanations without evidence.
Now I think you should have at least three.

Besides that it's more fun, that's what it means to lack evidence, right?
As evidence accumulates, all explanations but one are eventually falsified.

I do sometimes propose bullshit explanations adversarially, when I'm annoyed, as Epicurus was, at the unfounded claims of others.
When I say you lack evidence, the content of my claim is that you cannot rule out other plausible alternatives.
The right way to make that case, at least in my opinion, is directly: list the alternatives.
Everything else (you haven't done a study, or you did a study but it wasn't randomized, etc. etc.) is at best a proxy for this substance.

But, again, it's fun to propose explanations.
When someone isn't taking what they're saying too seriously, I think I should meet them in the same spirit and play the same game.

## Recognizing evidence by the possibilities it excludes

Of course, sometimes a claim is well-founded after all.

Epicurus was not always able to notice well-supported claims.
His letter to Pythocles looks great as long as he's talking about the weather, where anything goes at an ancient Greek level of evidence.
But the letter looks bad whenever it touches on astronomy, where ancient Greek knowledge was impressive.

For example, Epicurus says that maybe the moon has its own light, and maybe it reflects the light of the sun.
Reasonable, right?
But if it's reflected sunlight, then the illuminated part of the moon should always be facing the sun, whereas if the moon simply glows, this would be an extraordinary coincidence.

Not only did the ancient Greek astronomers know that the moon was reflecting sunlight, but Aristarchus was able to determine, from the pattern of illumination, that the sun must be much farther from us than the moon.
Since they appear the same size in the sky, the sun must therefore be much larger.
Steven Weinberg's _To Explain the World_ has a great explanation of Aristarchus's argument.

Whereas Epicurus tells us that maybe the true sizes of the celestial bodies match their apparent size or maybe not.
And that maybe eclipses are the light going out or maybe it's just being blocked.
And, furthermore, that we shouldn't let ourselves be intimidated by astronomers.

Well, sure, we shouldn't listen to someone just because they do fancy math.
But the ancient Greek astronomers had the real thing: observations that perfectly fit one possibility but not another.
Not that they contradict the other, but it would require an extraordinary coincidence.

But I still think the ensemble of explanations was the right place to start.
That's what evidence is: it's what rules out the other explanations.
The best way to see that is directly; every other standard of evidence is just a proxy.
